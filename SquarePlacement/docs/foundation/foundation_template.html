Conditions and the Map

The algorithm keeps two descriptions of one situation: the map (cells that are free, chosen or blocked, and the pixels they cover) and the .conditions algebra (each free cell's set of terms). This draft puts the two side by side for every notion, from a single condition up to a refutation. The n-ary step is left out.

Every map and every term below is computed from the code: conditions.initial_map and bruteforce.compute_conditions, by docs/foundation/examples.py. Statements are marked in code when the code does this today and proposed when it is a design step still to be made.

1What are .conditions?

A cell (r, c) is the position of one 2×2 square; its square covers the pixels (r..r+1, c..c+1). In the maps, cells are boxes and pixels are the grid points: a cell covers the four grid points at its corners. The four cells around a grid point form a 2×2 block; they are the only cells that can cover that pixel.

On the map

If the three other corners of a block around y are blocked, only y can still cover the block's pixel, so y must be chosen. A corner is blocked by a chosen diagonal neighbour. Each corner gets one such blocker, and the blockers obey the rules a placement must obey anyway: no two are diagonal to each other, none sits in the block, and none is a diagonal neighbour of y (it would block y itself).

In .conditions

(3,3).forced_by ∋ {(1,2), (3,1), (4,3)}
read: chosen(1,2) ∧ chosen(3,1) ∧ chosen(4,3) ⇒ chosen(3,3)

y.forced_by is a set of terms. A term is a set of member ids; when all members are chosen, y must be chosen. Logically each term is a Horn clause with only positive literals chosen(·).

In the vacuum every interior cell has terms: four blocks around it, every admissible choice of blockers, a triple serving two blocks counted once. Terms with an off-board member are dropped, so cells near the edge have fewer.

A term names the blockers, never the corners. Which corner a member blocks, and which block the term came from, is not stored. This matters in §3 and §4.

2Placing (choosing) a cell

On the map

Choosing p places its square. A direct neighbour may be chosen too: the two squares overlap along a whole edge and form a 3×2 or 2×3 shape. A diagonal neighbour may not: the two squares would overlap in a single pixel. So the moment p is chosen, its four diagonal neighbours are blocked (§3).

In .conditions

(2,5): {(1,3), (2,3), (4,4)} → {(1,3), (2,3)}
a member that is chosen drops out; the term survives, one shorter

Wherever p is a member, it drops out. On this board terms contain (4,4); after the choice all of them are pairs. Chosen means member satisfied.

p's own terms are cleared: a chosen cell has nothing left to be forced into. A term that loses its last member is true: its cell is forced (§5). in code compute_conditions.

3The four blocked cells of a placed cell

On the map

A blocked cell can never be chosen while p stays chosen, and placements are not undone, so it is blocked for good. Its pixels can still be covered by other cells. Blocked says only that this cell's square will never be placed.

In .conditions

(1,2): {(0,0), (1,0), (3,3)} → dropped
a member that is blocked can never be chosen: the term can never become true

Wherever a blocked cell is a member, the term is dead and dropped. On this board terms have one of p's four diagonals as a member. The blocked cells' own terms are cleared as well ( terms, together with p's). Blocked means member impossible.

Chosen and blocked are the two ways a member leaves a term: chosen, and the term shrinks; blocked, and the term dies.

After that, a blocked cell is never named in any term. As a member it has removed terms; as a corner it was never named at all (§1). So a blocked cell has no id in the algebra. It exists only through what it did to the terms.

4A blocked 2×2 block, and why it is impossible

On the map

If all four cells of a block are blocked, the block's pixel can never be covered: a hole one pixel in size, the 1×1 defect. The tiling can never be completed there, so this state must never be reached.

Legal play does not walk into it: once three corners are blocked, the fourth is a seat and gets chosen, and that blocks the fourth blocker. The seat rule exists to avoid the hole.

In .conditions

There is no term for this outcome today. A term always ends in a cell to choose; a hole has no cell. When a forced cell gets blocked, compute_conditions clears that cell's terms, including the one that had just become true: the evidence is dropped.

defect term of the block around pixel (3,3):
{(3,1), (1,2), (4,3), (2,4)} → ⊥
one blocker per corner, no two diagonal to each other

proposed The defect term {m₁, m₂, m₃, m₄} → ⊥ describes the hole in the language of terms: when all four members are chosen, the state is impossible. In the vacuum each block has of them. It belongs to the block (the pixel), not to a cell.

It is the other half of a seat term. If {m₁, m₂, m₃} → y holds and m₄ is a cell that blocks y, then {m₁, m₂, m₃, m₄} → ⊥: choosing m₄ would block the cell the seat needs.

5Seat rule and forcing, versus collapse and links

The same fact twice: on the map, a seat forces a cell at the moment its third corner is blocked. In the terms, the condition collapses one member at a time, and its last-but-one stage, the single, is a link: the seat one placement in advance.

On the map

Seat rule: a block with three corners blocked and one free: the free cell must be chosen. It acts on the present state, when the third corner gets blocked. Before that, the map shows only partly blocked blocks.

6Paths and aliasing

On the map

In .conditions

A path is a set of cells connected by links. The aliases of Z are Z and every cell reachable from Z over links: chosen(Z) ⇒ chosen(each alias). Here: {(5,4), (3,5), (7,6), (7,8)}.

Aliasing is one-way. (3,5) chosen does not force Z. A mutual link, like (7,6) ⇄ (7,8), makes two cells equivalent: either one chosen means both. A path can fan out (Z has two links) and can contain cycles.

7Candidate placement and its consequence

On the map

A candidate is a free cell considered as a decision. In the pyramid it is a free cell of a placement tile, and each placement tile makes one decision. Its consequence: Z and all its aliases are chosen, and their diagonal neighbours are blocked. Here, settling the map after choosing Z places exactly the aliases (3,5), (7,6), (7,8), nothing more.

In .conditions

For the terms, the consequence of a candidate is two lists: the alias set (members that drop out of terms) and the ring of cells they block (members whose terms die).

The map's links change: links before, after. The new ones are the candidate's consequences for others: cells that are now one placement away from a seat.

In general, settling can force more than the aliases: two aliases can together complete a pair term. That is the n-ary step, outside this document.

8Blocked pairs

On the map

Each candidate is fine alone. Together they cannot be chosen: they are a blocked pair, like two diagonal neighbours but at a distance.

In .conditions

No .conditions term says this today. The pair is recorded in the tiles' .blocked lists as (Z₁, Z₂). in code Base.update_blocked

defect term, block around pixel (6,4):
{(4,2), (4,5), (7,5), (7,2)} → ⊥
(4,5), (7,5) chosen: they drop out
{(4,2), (7,2)} = {Z₁, Z₂} → ⊥

proposed As a defect term, the blocked pair is that term reduced to two members, both candidates: ¬(chosen(Z₁) ∧ chosen(Z₂)). It has the same shape as the exclusion of two diagonal neighbours, which is the primitive rule behind "blocked" (§2).

9Refutation

On the map

Z forces two cells that block each other, so choosing Z leads to a hole: Z can never be chosen. Everything the links derive past this point assumes both cells chosen, and is worthless. in code chains stop at their first contradiction (Base.chain_until_contradiction, and the n-ary step).

In .conditions

{(2,4), (2,7), (5,5), (3,4)} → ⊥
(2,4), (2,7) chosen: they drop out
{(5,5), (3,4)} → ⊥
(3,4) is an alias of Z = (5,5)
{Z} → ⊥ : Z is refuted

proposed A refutation of Z is a defect term all of whose remaining members are aliases of Z. The block around pixel (4,4) gives a second one: {(2,4), (5,2), Z, (4,5)}.

It is permanent. More placements only shrink and kill terms. The links Z → (3,4) and Z → (4,5) were derived from cells that stay chosen. If one of (3,4), (4,5) gets chosen from elsewhere, it blocks the other, which Z still forces. If one gets blocked, Z forces a blocked cell. Either way it is the hole again. A refuted Z stays refuted in every later state.

proposed So a refuted Z is blocked: a defect term reduced to {Z} has exactly the effect a chosen diagonal neighbour has. Every term with Z as a member dies. This gives blocked cells two origins, both explained:

Open questions

  1. Where defect terms live (per block, i.e. per pixel), and how compute_conditions reduces them: the same rules as for conditions, with ⊥ in place of a cell.
  2. Whether a refuted candidate is blocked for good rather than suspended for one pass. The permanence argument in §9 holds as long as the real map and the rules stay fixed during a run.
  3. Blocks outside the real map need no cover, so they get no defect terms. The margin's half- and quarter-cells need the same care as in Base.restrict_to_real_map.
  4. Blocked pairs: whether a defect term reduced to two candidates should be stored as a term or stay a .blocked entry.
